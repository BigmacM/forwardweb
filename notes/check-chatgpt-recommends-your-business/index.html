How to check whether ChatGPT recommends your business

You can find out in twenty minutes, for free, without any tools. This is the method I use before I quote anyone for AI visibility work.

Maksut Ozcan
Founder, ForwardWebPublished Nov 2, 2026Updated Nov 2, 20265 min read
On this pageWhy one question isn’t enoughThe 20-minute methodHow to read the answersWhat to fix firstWhat this method can’t tell you
The short version
  • Ask the questions your customers ask, not your company name.
  • Run each question twice on two assistants and log who is named.
  • Absent means fix your basics first. Named but wrong means fix your facts.

Most owners check by typing their business name into ChatGPT. That tells you whether it has heard of you. It does not tell you whether it would recommend you to a stranger, and a stranger is who brings in new customers.

The better test is to ask what a customer asks, before they know your name. This note is the method I use. It takes about twenty minutes, it costs nothing, and you do not need a tool.

Why one question isn’t enough

Assistants do not give the same answer every time. Ask the same question twice and you can get two different lists. A single mention tells you very little, and so does a single miss.

That is why the method below uses a small repeated sample. You are not looking for a verdict from one answer. You are looking for a pattern across several.

Two other things are worth knowing before you start.

  • Wording changes the answer. “Best plumber in Vaughan” and “who should I call for a burst pipe in Vaughan” can name different businesses. Use the phrasing your real customers use.
  • Your own history can colour the result. If you are signed in, or you have searched for your own business many times, the answer may lean towards what the assistant already knows about you. A private window and a fresh chat give a fairer picture.

The 20-minute method

  1. Write five questions a customer would type before choosing you. Include your city and your service, and leave out your brand name. One question about the problem (“my basement floods when it rains, who fixes that near Vaughan?”), one about the service (“emergency plumber Vaughan”), one about price (“how much does a plumber cost in Vaughan?”), one comparing options (“how do I choose a plumber?”), and one specific to something you do better than others.
  2. Ask each question twice on two assistants. ChatGPT is the obvious one. Use Google’s AI answers or another assistant for the second. Open a new chat for every run, in a private window, so earlier answers do not carry over.
  3. Log every run. A spreadsheet is enough. One row per run, with these columns: the question, the assistant, the run number, whether you were named, in what position, and what it said about you.
  4. Note the sources. Some assistants show the pages they drew on. When yours does, write down which sites were cited, because those are the sites shaping the answer.
  5. Repeat the exercise in a month. One snapshot is a baseline. Two snapshots show direction.

That is twenty runs in total, five questions across two assistants and two runs each. It sounds like a lot, but each run takes under a minute.

How to read the answers

When the log is done, count the runs where you were named, and look at how you were described. Most results fall into one of three shapes.

Never namedThe assistants cannot read you, or cannot tell what you doCrawler access, homepage opener
Named, details wrongYour facts conflict across the webListings and directory consistency
Named, after competitorsKnown, but not cited as an authorityDirect answers, authorship, reviews

Be careful with the second row. If an assistant gets your hours, address or services wrong, it usually found conflicting versions somewhere. An old directory listing, a previous address on a social profile and a page on your own site that was never updated can all do it. The fix is boring and effective: pick one correct set of facts and make every listing match.

Being named once is luck. Being named in most of the runs is a position.

What to fix first

If you were never named, do not start by rewriting your site. Start with the cheapest check, which is whether AI crawlers are allowed in at all. A single misplaced line in robots.txt can block them. I wrote a free tool that checks whether your robots.txt is blocking AI crawlers, and it takes a minute. If crawlers are blocked, nothing else you do will matter until that is fixed.

After that, work through the basics in roughly this order.

  1. Say what you do in the first paragraph of your homepage. What, where and for whom, in two plain sentences. Assistants quote openings, and vague openers get skipped.
  2. Answer the questions buyers ask, on the page. Price, process and timing, in short direct sentences. These are the sentences an assistant can lift.
  3. Make your facts identical everywhere. Name, address and phone should match on your site, your Google Business Profile and every directory.
  4. Put a named author and a date on what you publish. Anonymous, undated pages are weaker sources.
  5. Set up measurement. In Google Analytics 4 you can see visits that arrive from AI assistants as their own source, if you look for them. If you cannot see them, you cannot tell whether any of this is working.

What this method can’t tell you

It is a snapshot of a moving target. Assistants change, and so do their answers, so treat the log as a dated record and not as a permanent grade. It also cannot tell you how many people asked. A strong result on a question nobody types is not worth much, which is why the first step is writing questions your customers would genuinely use.
